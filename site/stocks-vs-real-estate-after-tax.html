Stocks vs. Real Estate: Which Actually Delivers After Tax?

Eight percent from the market and eight percent from an apartment deal are not the same eight percent.

David Snider / 9 July 2025 / 5 min read

What is better, 8% in the stock market or 8% from a multifamily deal? Most people would say they are identical. After tax, they are not, and the gap is where the real story sits.

On paper they look the same

Invest $100,000 at 8% in either, and you would expect $8,000 a year from both. Here is what happens once tax is applied.

The stock side

Income from equities is generally taxed in one of three ways: dividends taxed annually at ordinary or qualified rates, capital gains taxed on sale, and interest income taxed as ordinary income.

Take $8,000 in dividends. In a 24% bracket that is $1,920 in tax, leaving $6,080. Your true after-tax yield is 6.08%, and that is before brokerage costs, volatility and inflation.

The multifamily side

Now the same $100,000 in a multifamily deal yielding 8%. You receive $8,000 in cash. Your K-1 reports a paper loss from depreciation. Depending on the deal and your circumstances, some or all of that $8,000 may be offset.

The point is not that tax disappears. It is that the same headline yield can arrive with a very different tax character.

The limitation that changes this for most people

Depreciation from a passive investment generally cannot shelter your salary. Under the passive activity loss rules it offsets passive income, and the excess is suspended and carried forward. Any comparison that skips this is overstating the result. Ours does not.

Why the difference compounds

In investing it is not what you earn, it is what you keep. Deferred tax is capital that stays invested and keeps working, and over a full hold period that difference compounds rather than simply adding up.

  • Defer income tax rather than paying it annually
  • Compound the deferred amount instead of remitting it
  • Use depreciation to create legitimate paper losses
  • Reinvest the tax saved into further assets
David SniderManaging Partner, Chateau Capital

I used to think I was doing well earning 8 to 10% in mutual funds, until I worked out how much I was losing to tax every year, and how little control I had.

When I moved to multifamily my headline returns were similar, but my after-tax income was not. And I could predict it. Real assets, rent rolls, depreciation, all of it backed by the tax code.

That is one reason I built Chateau Capital. I wanted to help other people keep more of what they earn, without the market drama.

Bracket and yield figures above are worked examples chosen for clarity, not projections. Your outcome depends on your own tax position, the specific deal and current law.
